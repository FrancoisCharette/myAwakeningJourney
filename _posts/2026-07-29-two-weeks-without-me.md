title: "The two weeks my body carried on without me"

On July 29th 2022, a car ran a red light and hit my motorcycle at an intersection less than a mile from my house. I have no memory of that day. I have no memory of the two weeks that followed either. Everything I am about to tell you about those two weeks comes from the police report and
from my family, because I was not there to witness any of it.

That sentence is the reason this site exists, and I want to be precise about what I mean by it. I do not mean I was unconscious in a hospital bed. My body was awake. It walked, eventually. It answered questions. It sat in the living room. My family spent two weeks with it. **What was not there was me.**

## The crack, ten months earlier

I should start before the accident, because the accident did not begin my awakening — it demonstrated something that had already been nagging at me.

I came to YouTube late, around 2015, and for the first few years I used it strictly for work: computing metrics, coding, design methods. Over time the recommendations pulled me sideways into science, astronomy, archaeology, politics, film. Then in September 2021, entirely by chance, I landed on a Sadhguru video titled *"I am Not the Body, I am Not the Mind."*

Normally I would have given something like that ninety seconds and moved on. But I was in a bad period — severe anxiety, all kinds of crazy thoughts arriving for no reason at all. Suffering, as they say. So I watched the whole thing.

I would not claim it changed my life that day. What it did was smaller and more durable: it let me detect a small crack in my ego. Not a revelation. A crack. I went on watching a spiritual video maybe two or three times a month and otherwise carried on exactly as before. My awakening was instigated at that point. It had not started.

## What five witnesses saw

On Friday July 29th 2022, around 5:00 p.m., I rode to a sub shop to pick up dinner. On the way back I stopped at a red light at a large intersection — two lanes north, two south, three east, three west — in dense California Friday traffic.

According to five witnesses, when the light turned green I moved forward and a car crossing on the red hit my motorcycle. I was thrown off, flipped over the car, and landed motionless on the far side. The motorcycle was totaled, so it was a hard hit.

The medical scans found two brain bleeds, one on the surface of the brain and one deep inside. By luck, the blood was not pooling anywhere, which would have been a much bigger problem. I arrived at the trauma center as a John Doe.

My wife is handicapped and cannot drive. When I was late coming home she tried calling, and after half an hour she asked my daughter — who happened to be visiting that week — to go look for me. It did not take my daughter long to find an accident at a busy intersection a mile from the house, and to recognize the motorcycle on the side of the road as mine. The police showed her a driver's license photo. She identified me and drove to the trauma center.

When she found me, a doctor was working through the standard questions they ask someone with that kind of injury, and I was completely unresponsive. My daughter took my hand, said hello, and asked me how I was — in French, my native language. I squeezed her hand. The doctor asked her to put the standard questions to me in French, and apparently I answered most of them correctly. He told her that reverting to your first language is normal with this type of trauma, and that recovery would take time but should be complete. He was right.

The intensive care unit was crowded, and the next day, after more scans, the doctor asked my daughter whether she could simply take me home — there was nothing more they could do for me there. I am a two-hundred-pound man who could not stand up straight. Two nurses helped load me into the car. At the house, my wife and daughter moved me from the car onto my wife's electric scooter, drove me to the bedroom, and dragged me into bed.

For the first two or three days, they tell me, I was like a baby. I could not walk. I could not drink from a glass without choking, so my wife gave me wet towels to suck on. I ate nothing. My eyes drifted independently of each other without settling on anything. My brain was re-learning balance, motor control, swallowing, eye coordination — what the doctors call neuroplasticity, and what felt, from the outside, like a body rewiring itself from scratch.

## Coming back, with a fork in my hand

The first thing I remember is a piece of watermelon.

My body was sitting in the living room watching television, eating pre-cut watermelon. I have a clear memory of holding the fork, about to eat, and thinking: *Wow, this is great, I could do this for the rest of my life.* Sitting down, eating something, watching TV.

Too many thoughts arrived at once for me to reconstruct them all. But one was unmistakable: I knew I had been away. Not away as in asleep — I knew, with a certainty I cannot justify, that it had been much longer than a night.

I could hear my wife typing on her computer and my daughter on the phone upstairs. I got up and went to say hello to my wife, because I had not seen her in a long time.

She asked me whether I needed something.

## The part I still cannot get past

That is the detail that has stayed with me ever since the accident, and it is the real subject of this post.

Nobody noticed.

For two weeks my family had been living with my body, caring for it, speaking to it, and getting answers back — and not one of them registered that the person they were talking to was not there. Later that first day back I heard my wife and daughter discussing the accident, started asking questions, and was handed the police report. That is how I learned my son had flown in from out of state and spent most of those two weeks in our house. He had left the morning before. I have no recollection of him being there at all.

The only difference my wife had noticed was that I was not talkative. I answered when spoken to. I never started a conversation, never asked anything, never volunteered. For a man recovering from a traumatic brain injury, that seemed entirely normal to everyone.

I recovered. Two months of physiotherapy, and medically I am considered fully healed. My shoulders still hurt and still have less strength and range than they should — they went from roughly half their normal range to nearly all of it, and they have kept improving slowly, year over year.

But the accident left something else. Before it, *"I am Not the Body, I am Not the Mind"* was an idea I had heard and found interesting. After it, it was not an idea any more. My body had run for two weeks without me and passed for me convincingly enough that the people who love me most did not detect the difference. I did not reason my way to that conclusion. It was demonstrated to me, and I was not even present for the demonstration.

I do not have this fully worked out. That is the point of writing here instead of recording it — I expect to revise this page as I understand it better.

The question I keep returning to is the one my wife's ordinary "do you need something?" opened up: **how much of an ordinary day is run by that same autopilot, with nobody home and nobody noticing?** I have thought about it a great deal.

If you have experienced something similar, or if you think there is a simpler explanation for what happened to me than the one I am drawn to, I genuinely want to hear it. That is what the comments below are for.
